You almost never measure everyone you want to say something about. You measure
a sample and generalise. Whether that generalisation holds depends far more on
*how* the sample was drawn than on how large it is.

## Population, frame and sample

- The **population** is everyone your conclusion is about: all BMS bachelor
  students this year, say.
- The **sampling frame** is the list you actually draw from: the student
  administration's mailing list. People missing from the frame can never end up
  in the sample.
- The **sample** is who you invite, and the **response rate** is the share of
  them who actually take part. If 400 are invited and 120 respond, the response
  rate is 30%.

## Probability and non-probability samples

In a **probability sample** every unit in the frame has a known chance, larger
than zero, of being selected.

- A **simple random sample** gives every unit the same chance, like drawing
  names from a hat.
- A **stratified sample** first splits the population into groups (strata),
  such as programmes, and draws at random within each, so every group is
  represented in a fixed number.
- A **cluster sample** draws whole groups at random, such as tutorial groups,
  and measures everyone in the chosen groups.

In a **non-probability sample** the chances are unknown.

- A **convenience sample** takes whoever is easy to reach.
- A **purposive sample** picks units deliberately for a reason, such as
  experts.
- A **snowball sample** asks participants to recruit others.
- A **quota sample** fills fixed numbers per group, like stratified sampling,
  but without drawing at random within the groups.

## Seeing the difference in R

The course dataset `wellbeing-population.csv` holds all 5000 students of an
imaginary programme pair, so here you can compare samples with the truth.

<CodeBlock id="c-population" code={`library(dplyr)

population <- read.csv("data/wellbeing-population.csv", stringsAsFactors = TRUE)
mean(population$stress)`} />

A simple random sample of 100 lands close to that mean.

<CodeBlock id="c-random" code={`set.seed(1)
random_sample <- population %>% slice_sample(n = 100)
mean(random_sample$stress)`} />

Now a convenience sample: you hand out questionnaires at an 8:45 lecture, and
only the students who slept at least seven hours made it there.

<CodeBlock id="c-convenience" code={`set.seed(1)
lecture_sample <- population %>%
  filter(sleep_hours >= 7) %>%
  slice_sample(n = 100)
mean(lecture_sample$stress)`} />

Repeating each method many times shows the pattern.

<CodeBlock id="c-repeat" code={`set.seed(2)
random_means <- replicate(500, mean(slice_sample(population, n = 100)$stress))
rested <- population %>% filter(sleep_hours >= 7)
lecture_means <- replicate(500, mean(slice_sample(rested, n = 100)$stress))

c(true = mean(population$stress), random = mean(random_means), lecture = mean(lecture_means))`} />

The random samples scatter around the true mean: that scatter is **sampling
error**, and it shrinks as samples get larger. The lecture samples scatter
around a value about two points too low: that is **bias**, a systematic error
that no sample size removes. Nonresponse works the same way when the people who
do not respond differ from those who do.

A stratified sample draws a fixed number from each group. In dplyr, group first
and then draw.

<CodeBlock id="c-stratified" code={`set.seed(3)
per_programme <- population %>%
  group_by(programme) %>%
  slice_sample(n = 20) %>%
  ungroup()
count(per_programme, programme)`} />
