Leaving rates ranged from 16% to 31% across the four departments. If department
had nothing to do with leaving, the rates would still differ a little from
sample to sample. The chi-square test asks whether they differ more than that.

<CodeBlock id="c-load" code={`d <- read.csv("data/workplace.csv", stringsAsFactors = TRUE)

counts <- table(d$department, d$left_company)
counts`} />

## What "no relationship" would look like

If department and leaving were independent, every department would lose the
same share of its people: the overall rate, 109 out of 480. Engineering has 128
people, so it would expect 128 × 109 / 480 = 29.1 leavers. In general, each
cell expects its row total times its column total, divided by N.

<CodeBlock id="c-expected" code={`expected <- outer(rowSums(counts), colSums(counts)) / sum(counts)
round(expected, 1)`} />

Expected counts do not have to be whole numbers. They are averages over all the
samples the null hypothesis could produce.

## One number for the whole table

For each cell, take the gap between observed and expected, square it and scale
it by the expected count. The sum over all cells is the **chi-square
statistic**.

<CodeBlock id="c-by-hand" code={`x2 <- sum((counts - expected)^2 / expected)
x2`} />

Squaring makes every gap count, whichever way it goes. Dividing by the expected
count means a gap of 8 matters more in a cell that expected 20 than in one that
expected 100.

## The test

`chisq.test()` does all of the above and compares the statistic with its null
distribution, a chi-square distribution with (rows - 1) × (columns - 1) degrees
of freedom.

<CodeBlock id="c-test" code={`test <- chisq.test(counts)
test

test$expected`} />

X-squared = 9.94 with 3 degrees of freedom, p = .019: department and leaving
are related in this sample by more than chance would usually produce.

`chisq.test()` also returns standardized residuals: the gap between observed and
expected, scaled so that under independence each behaves roughly like a z-score. Values beyond about ±2 mark the cells that drive the result.

<CodeBlock id="c-residuals" code={`round(test$stdres, 2)`} />

Support lost more people than independence predicts, and Sales fewer.

For a 2 by 2 table, `chisq.test()` applies Yates' continuity correction by
default, which makes the test slightly more cautious. Both versions are
reported in the literature; say which one you used.

## How strong is it?

The chi-square statistic grows with the sample size, so a big one does not mean
a big effect. **Cramér's V** rescales it to run from 0, no association, to 1,
where one variable fully predicts the other.

<CodeBlock id="c-cramer" code={`n <- sum(counts)
k <- min(dim(counts))

v <- sqrt(test$statistic / (n * (k - 1)))
unname(v)`} />

Rough benchmarks for a table with two columns: .10 is small, .30 medium, .50
large. They are a starting point, not a verdict; what counts as large depends
on the field.

## When the test cannot be trusted

The chi-square distribution is an approximation, and it needs enough data.
The usual rule is an **expected** count of at least 5 in every cell, or at least
in 80% of them, none below 1. `chisq.test()` warns you ("Chi-squared approximation may be incorrect") as soon
as any expected count is below 5, which is stricter than the 80% rule.
Then use Fisher's exact test, which makes no such approximation.

<CodeBlock id="c-fisher" code={`small <- d[d$site == "Hengelo" & d$department %in% c("Marketing", "Sales"), ]
small_counts <- table(droplevels(small$department), small$left_company)
small_counts

fisher.test(small_counts)`} />

In this small table of 29 people, the two "left" cells expect fewer than 4
leavers each, so the chi-square approximation is off the table. Fisher's test
gives p = .68: nothing to see here, and with so few people there rarely would be.

The test also needs each person counted once, in one cell. A table of 480
answers from 120 people, each asked four times, breaks that. Modules 14 and 15
build the models that handle repeated answers.
