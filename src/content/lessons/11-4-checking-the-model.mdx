Every p-value and confidence interval in this module rests on assumptions:
independent cases, a straight-line relationship, and residuals that are equally
spread and roughly normal. It also helps if the fit is not driven by a handful of
cases. Lesson 10-3 checked them by eye in one line.
This lesson checks them properly, and shows what a failed check can tell you.

<CodeBlock id="c-load" code={`library(dplyr)
library(broom)
library(ggplot2)

d <- read.csv("data/workplace.csv", stringsAsFactors = TRUE)

model <- lm(wellbeing ~ autonomy + workload + tenure_years, data = d)
checked <- augment(model, data = d)

checked %>% select(employee_id, wellbeing, .fitted, .resid, .cooksd) %>% head()`} />

`augment()` returns one row per employee, with the fitted value, the residual and
Cook's distance added as columns next to the original ones.

## Residuals against fitted values

If the model is right, the residuals scatter evenly around zero at every fitted
value, with no curve and no funnel.

<CodeBlock id="c-resid-fitted" code={`ggplot(checked, aes(x = .fitted, y = .resid)) +

  geom_smooth(se = FALSE, method = "loess", formula = y ~ x) +
  labs(x = "Fitted wellbeing", y = "Residual") +
  theme_classic()`} />

No curve and no funnel, but something else: a long tail of large negative
residuals below the cloud, and nothing like it above.

## Are the residuals normal?

A **Q-Q plot** puts the residuals against the values a normal distribution would
give. Normal residuals fall on the line.

<CodeBlock id="c-qq" code={`ggplot(checked, aes(sample = .resid)) +
  stat_qq(alpha = 0.4) +
  stat_qq_line() +
  labs(x = "Theoretical quantiles", y = "Residuals") +
  theme_classic()`} />

<CodeBlock id="c-who" code={`checked %>%
  filter(.resid < -15) %>%
  count(department)`} />

Every one of them is in Engineering. Module 3 found the same thing from the
other side: Engineering's mean sits below its median because a minority of
engineers do very badly. The model has no variable for whatever that is (an
on-call rotation, say), so it cannot predict it, and the misfit lands in the
residuals.

A failed check is often a finding, not a nuisance. Here it points to a missing
predictor, and the real fix is to measure it. Adding department helps, but
cannot remove the tail, because most engineers are not affected.

<CodeBlock id="c-department" code={`with_department <- lm(wellbeing ~ autonomy + workload + tenure_years + department, data = d)

bind_rows(
  glance(model) %>% mutate(model = "without department"),
  glance(with_department) %>% mutate(model = "with department")
) %>% select(model, r.squared, sigma)`} />

With 480 people the slopes and their intervals are still trustworthy despite
skewed residuals: the Central Limit Theorem of Module 6 looks after the sampling
distribution of each slope. Normality matters most in small samples.

## Influential cases

A case is **influential** when leaving it out moves the fitted line. **Cook's
distance** measures that, one number per case. A common rule of thumb flags
cases above 4 / n for a closer look.

<CodeBlock id="c-cooks" code={`checked %>%
  arrange(desc(.cooksd)) %>%
  select(employee_id, department, autonomy, workload, wellbeing, .resid, .cooksd) %>%
  head(5)`} />

The top case has the maximum autonomy of 10 and very low wellbeing: an unusual
position on x (leverage) together with a big miss on y, which is what gives a
point influence over the line.

<Exercise id="m11-4-a" />

What to do with them is a matter of reporting, not deleting. Refit without the
most influential case and see whether the conclusion changes. If it does not,
say so in one sentence. If it does, report both.

<Exercise id="m11-4-b" />

Leaving out every flagged case at once is a different matter.

<CodeBlock id="c-trim" code={`trimmed <- lm(wellbeing ~ autonomy + workload + tenure_years,
              data = checked %>% filter(.cooksd <= 4 / n()))

bind_rows(
  tidy(model) %>% mutate(fit = "all 480"),
  tidy(trimmed) %>% mutate(fit = "flagged cases removed")
) %>%
  filter(term == "autonomy") %>%
  select(fit, estimate, std.error)`} />

The autonomy slope jumps by more than half, because most of the removed cases
are the struggling engineers, and removing them rewrites the population. Cook's
distance tells you where to look, not what to throw away.

## When the assumptions fail badly

- **Curve in the residuals:** the relationship is not a straight line. Add a
  squared term, `I(x^2)`, or transform a skewed predictor with `log()`.
- **Funnel:** the spread grows with the fitted values. Transforming a skewed,
  positive outcome with `log()` often fixes it, and robust standard errors
  (the `sandwich` package, in RStudio) keep the slopes and repair their SEs.
- **Small sample, clearly non-normal residuals:** bootstrap the interval.
  Resample employees with replacement, refit, and take the middle 95% of the
  slopes.

<CodeBlock id="c-boot" code={`set.seed(114)
slopes <- replicate(1000, {
  rows <- sample(nrow(d), replace = TRUE)
  coef(lm(wellbeing ~ autonomy + workload + tenure_years, data = d[rows, ]))[["autonomy"]]
})

rbind(
  bootstrap = quantile(slopes, c(0.025, 0.975)),
  usual = confint(model)["autonomy", ]
)`} />

The two intervals nearly agree, which is the Central Limit Theorem at work
again. With 30 people they might not.

For a simple comparison of two groups there are rank-based tests too:
`wilcox.test()` for two groups and `kruskal.test()` for several. They answer a
slightly different question (about ranks, not means), so use them when the
question allows it, not as an automatic escape.
