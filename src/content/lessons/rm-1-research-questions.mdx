Statistics answers questions, but only some kinds of question. Before any data
is collected, a study needs a question that data can actually answer. This
module covers the research methods that come before the analysis: what makes a
question empirical, who and what you measure, who ends up in the sample, when a
relationship can be read as cause and effect, and how a design protects that
reading.

## Three kinds of question

Not every question can be settled with data.

- A **normative** question asks what *should* be: "Should the university ban
  phones in lectures?" Data can inform it, but the answer also rests on values.
- A **conceptual** question asks what a word means: "What do we mean by
  student wellbeing?" It is settled by definition and argument.
- An **empirical** question asks what *is*: "Do students who use their phone in
  lectures score lower on the exam?" Observation can answer it.

Research in the social sciences starts from empirical questions, though a
normative question is often the reason the study is done at all.

## Describing or explaining

Empirical questions come in two kinds.

- A **descriptive** question asks how things are: "What share of BMS students
  sleeps less than seven hours?" It involves one variable, or several described
  side by side.
- An **explanatory** question asks why: "Does sleep explain differences in exam
  scores?" It links an independent variable (the possible cause) to a dependent
  variable (the outcome).

A good empirical research question names three things: the **units** (who or
what is studied), the **variables** (what is measured about them) and the
**setting** (where and when). "Do BMS students at the University of Twente who
sleep more score higher on their first statistics exam, in the 2025-2026
academic year?" names all three. "Does sleep matter?" names none of them.

## The wheel of science

Theory and observation feed each other in a cycle, sometimes drawn as the
**wheel of science**. Going from theory to a hypothesis to observation is
**deduction**: you derive what you expect to see and check it. Going from
observations to a general pattern to theory is **induction**. A hypothesis is a
testable statement about the expected relation, such as "students who sleep
more score higher on the exam", and it comes with a direction you can check.

Data can contradict a hypothesis but never prove a theory once and for all.
People tend to notice and remember the evidence that fits what they already
believe. That is **confirmation bias**, and it is the reason a study states its
hypothesis and its test before the data come in.

## Research for decisions

Organisations commission research to make better decisions. A policy decision
runs through phases, and research can support each one: describing the problem,
choosing between options, and evaluating the choice. An evaluation before a
policy starts, judging which option is likely to work, is an **ex ante**
evaluation. One while it runs, checking whether it is carried out as planned,
is a **process** evaluation. One afterwards, judging whether it worked, is an
**ex post** evaluation.
