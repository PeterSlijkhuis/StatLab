One proportion answers "how often". The more interesting question is usually
"how often, for whom": do some departments lose more people than others? That
takes two categorical variables at once, and the tool is a table of counts.

<CodeBlock id="c-load" code={`d <- read.csv("data/workplace.csv", stringsAsFactors = TRUE)

counts <- table(d$department, d$left_company)
counts`} />

Each cell counts the people with that combination: Engineering and stayed,
Engineering and left, and so on. The first variable you give `table()` goes
down the side, the second across the top. This is a **contingency table**, or a
cross-table.

## Totals

<CodeBlock id="c-margins" code={`addmargins(counts)`} />

The row totals are the department sizes and the column totals are how many
stayed and left overall. All the margins add up to 480.

## Proportions, and which way to divide

`prop.table()` turns counts into proportions, and its `margin` argument decides
what adds up to 1. The choice is the whole analysis.

<CodeBlock id="c-rows" code={`# margin = 1: each ROW adds up to 1. Of each department, what share left?
round(prop.table(counts, margin = 1), 3)`} />

<CodeBlock id="c-columns" code={`# margin = 2: each COLUMN adds up to 1. Of the leavers, which department were they in?
round(prop.table(counts, margin = 2), 3)`} />

The two tables answer different questions. Rows give each department's leaving
rate: Support loses about 31% of its people, Sales about 16%. Columns describe
the leavers: a third of everyone who left came from Support. Put the variable
that might do the influencing, department, in the rows and divide by rows, and
the table reads as a comparison of rates.

## A picture of it

A stacked bar where every bar reaches 100% shows the row proportions. Map the
outcome to `fill` and ask `geom_bar()` for `position = "fill"`.
