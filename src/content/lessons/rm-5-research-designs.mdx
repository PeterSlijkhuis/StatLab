The previous lesson checked for third variables after the data were in. A good
design rules many of them out before any data are collected. Designs are
written in a short notation:

- **X** is the intervention, or treatment.
- **O** is an observation, a measurement.
- **R** means the groups were formed by random assignment; **N** means they
  were not (a non-equivalent group, such as an existing class).

Time runs from left to right, and each line is one group.

## Designs from weak to strong

A **one-shot case study**, `X O`, measures once after the intervention. With
nothing to compare against, it shows almost nothing.

A **one-group pretest-posttest design**, `O X O`, measures before and after.
Any change could also come from something else that happened in between.

An **interrupted time series**, `O O O X O O O`, measures several times before
and after. A trend that was already there before X is then visible, and a jump
right after X is more convincing.

A **non-equivalent control group design** adds a comparison group that was not
randomised:

```
N  O  X  O
N  O     O
```

A **pretest-posttest control group design** assigns people to the groups at
random. This is the classic **experiment**:

```
R  O  X  O
R  O     O
```

Random assignment makes the groups equal *on average* in everything, measured
or not, so a difference afterwards can be put down to X or to chance, and
chance is what a statistical test measures. A **posttest-only control group
design**, `R X O` against `R O`, drops the pretest and avoids its side effects.

## Random assignment in R

`sample()` shuffles a vector. Shuffle a list of 240 "treatment" and 240
"control" labels, and each of 480 employees gets one at random.

<CodeBlock id="c-assign" code={`d <- read.csv("data/workplace.csv", stringsAsFactors = TRUE)

set.seed(4)
d$group <- sample(rep(c("treatment", "control"), each = nrow(d) / 2))
table(d$group)
aggregate(tenure_years ~ group, data = d, FUN = mean)`} />

The two groups differ a little in tenure, but only by chance. Compare that with
the `training` column in the same data: employees *chose* whether to follow the
training, so anything that drove that choice, such as motivation, travels along
with it. That is **self-selection**, and it is what random assignment removes.

## Validity and its threats

A study has **internal validity** when its conclusion about cause and effect
holds for the people studied. It has **external validity** when the conclusion
generalises to other people, settings and times. Random assignment protects
internal validity; random *sampling* (previous lesson) protects external
validity. They are different steps, and an experiment on first-year
psychology students can be internally valid and still say little about
employees.

The usual **threats to internal validity**, each a rival explanation for a
change from O to O:

- **History**: something else happened between the measurements, such as a
  news event.
- **Maturation**: participants changed by themselves, by getting older, more
  tired or more experienced.
- **Testing**: the first measurement affects the second, for example because
  people remember the questions.
- **Instrumentation**: the measuring instrument or the observer changed.
- **Regression to the mean**: a group selected for extreme scores scores less
  extreme the next time, even without any intervention.
- **Selection**: the groups differed before X, because they were not
  randomised.
- **Attrition**: participants drop out, and those who drop out differ from
  those who stay.
- **Social interaction**: groups learn from each other, compete, or the
  control group feels disadvantaged.

Experiments also guard against expectations. A **placebo** gives the control
group something that looks like the treatment but lacks its active part. In a
**double blind** study neither the participants nor the researchers who measure
them know who got what.
