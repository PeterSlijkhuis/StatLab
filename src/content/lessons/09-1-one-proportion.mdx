Everything since Module 5 has been about the mean of a number. Plenty of questions
are not about numbers at all. Did the employee leave? Do they work remotely?
Did the student pass? The answer is yes or no, and what you summarise is how
often it is yes.

<CodeBlock id="c-load" code={`d <- read.csv("data/workplace.csv", stringsAsFactors = TRUE)

table(d$left_company)`} />

## A proportion is a mean

`left_company` is coded 0 for stayed and 1 for left. The mean of a column of
zeros and ones is the share of ones, so the proportion who left is one line.

<CodeBlock id="c-mean" code={`mean(d$left_company)

sum(d$left_company) / nrow(d)`} />

About 23% of these 480 employees left. The same question about the whole
company, or about next year, is the population question, and the sample answer
comes with sampling error, exactly as a sample mean did in Module 6.

## The interval

The recipe from Module 7 carries over: estimate, plus and minus about two
standard errors.

<CodeBlock id="c-by-hand" code={`p <- mean(d$left_company)
n <- nrow(d)
se <- sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)

c(lower = p - 1.96 * se, upper = p + 1.96 * se)`} />

`prop.test()` builds a different interval, the Wilson score interval (with a
continuity correction by default), which behaves better than estimate ± 1.96 SE
when the proportion is close to 0 or 1 or the sample is small. It is not centred
exactly on *p*. Give it the number of yes answers and the number of cases.

<CodeBlock id="c-prop-test" code={`prop.test(sum(d$left_company), nrow(d))$conf.int`} />

The two intervals agree to within half a percentage point. With a sample this size the
choice of interval hardly matters; with 30 people it would.

## Testing against a known value

Suppose the sector reports that 30% of staff leave within the period this
dataset covers. Is this company different? `prop.test()` takes the value to
test against as `p`.

<CodeBlock id="c-benchmark" code={`prop.test(sum(d$left_company), nrow(d), p = 0.30)`} />

The logic is the null distribution of Module 8. If the true rate were 30%,
samples of 480 would rarely produce 23%, so the p-value is small. The test
statistic is called X-squared here, and Lesson 9-3 explains why.

For small samples, `binom.test()` gives the exact answer instead of an
approximation. Its arguments are the same.

<CodeBlock id="c-binom" code={`binom.test(sum(d$left_company), nrow(d), p = 0.30)`} />
