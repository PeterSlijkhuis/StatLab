Wellbeing, engagement and stress are not things you can measure with one
question. A questionnaire asks several, each answered on a scale such as 1 to 5,
and the score is the average of the answers. Before you average them, two
things need checking: that every item points the same way, and that the items
agree with each other well enough to be added up at all.

The course datasets hold finished scale scores, not the separate answers, so
this lesson builds a small survey of its own: five items, 200 people.

<CodeBlock id="c-survey" code={`set.seed(34)
n <- 200
trait <- rnorm(n)
likert <- function(x) pmin(5, pmax(1, round(3 + x)))
survey <- data.frame(
  q1 = likert(0.9 * trait + rnorm(n, 0, 0.6)),
  q2 = likert(0.8 * trait + rnorm(n, 0, 0.7)),
  q3 = likert(-0.8 * trait + rnorm(n, 0, 0.7)),
  q4 = likert(0.7 * trait + rnorm(n, 0, 0.8)),
  q5 = likert(0.1 * trait + rnorm(n, 0, 1))
)

head(survey)`} />

Imagine the items read like this, answered from 1, strongly disagree, to 5,
strongly agree:

| Item | Wording |
| --- | --- |
| q1 | I feel energetic at work. |
| q2 | I look forward to my working day. |
| q3 | I feel drained at the end of the day. |
| q4 | My work gives me satisfaction. |
| q5 | I like the coffee at work. |

## Items that point the wrong way

<CodeBlock id="c-cor" code={`round(cor(survey), 2)`} />

q1, q2 and q4 correlate positively with each other. q3 correlates negatively with
all of them, because agreeing with it means *low* wellbeing. Questionnaires mix
directions like this on purpose, so that people who agree with everything stand
out. Before averaging, a **reverse-worded** item has to be turned round.

On a 1 to 5 scale, 1 should become 5, 2 become 4, and so on: the reversed
value is (lowest + highest) minus the answer, here `6 - q3`.

<CodeBlock id="c-reverse" code={`survey$q3_r <- 6 - survey$q3

table(survey$q3, survey$q3_r)`} />

## The scale score

With every item pointing the same way, the score is the mean of the items for
each person. The mean rather than the sum keeps the score on the scale the
items were answered on, so 4 still means "agree".

<CodeBlock id="c-score" code={`survey$score <- rowMeans(survey[, c("q1", "q2", "q3_r", "q4", "q5")])

summary(survey$score)`} />

## Do the items belong together?

A scale score assumes the items measure one thing. If they do, people who score
high on one item tend to score high on the others, and the items' variation adds
up: the total varies much more than the items would if they were unrelated.
**Cronbach's alpha** turns that into one number, at most 1 and usually between 0 and 1.

<CodeBlock id="c-alpha" code={`items <- survey[, c("q1", "q2", "q3_r", "q4", "q5")]
k <- ncol(items)

item_variances <- sum(apply(items, 2, var))
total_variance <- var(rowSums(items))

k / (k - 1) * (1 - item_variances / total_variance)`} />

Alpha is 0.74. Rough conventions call .70 acceptable and .80 good for research
use. Alpha also rises with the number of items, so a long scale can reach .80
with items that agree only weakly.

<Exercise id="m3-4-b" />

## Which item is the weak one?

Recompute alpha with each item left out in turn. An item whose removal raises
alpha agrees poorly with the others, and is a candidate for not measuring the
same thing.

<CodeBlock id="c-drop" code={`alpha_of <- function(items) {
  k <- ncol(items)
  k / (k - 1) * (1 - sum(apply(items, 2, var)) / var(rowSums(items)))
}

sapply(names(items), function(item) alpha_of(items[, names(items) != item]))`} />

Dropping any of q1 to q4 lowers alpha. Dropping q5 raises it to 0.82, and its
correlations with the other items were close to zero. The coffee question does
not belong in a wellbeing scale. With a real questionnaire, you would look at
the wording before you drop anything, and you would report which items you
removed and why.

In RStudio, `psych::alpha(items)` does all of this in one call, and it warns
you when an item looks as if it still needs reversing.
