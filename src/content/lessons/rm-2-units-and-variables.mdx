A research question names units and variables. Data puts them in a grid: the
**data matrix**, with one row per unit and one column per variable. Every
dataset in this course is one, and so is every spreadsheet you will get from a
survey tool.

## Units of analysis and of observation

The **unit of analysis** is what your conclusions are about: students,
employees, schools, countries. The **unit of observation** is what you actually
measure. They often coincide, but not always. You can survey individual
employees (observation) to compare departments (analysis) by averaging within
each department.

Moving between levels has a trap. If countries with more internet users also
have higher average incomes, that does not show that the individuals who use
the internet are the richer ones. Concluding something about individuals from
data about groups is the **ecological fallacy**.

## Levels of measurement

How you may summarise a variable depends on its **level of measurement**. The
course uses the mnemonic **D-NOIR**:

- **Dichotomous**: two categories, such as passed or failed.
- **Nominal**: categories without an order, such as eye colour or programme.
- **Ordinal**: ordered categories without equal steps, such as "never,
  sometimes, often" or a ranking.
- **Interval**: equal steps but no true zero, such as temperature in degrees
  Celsius or a score on a 1 to 7 scale.
- **Ratio**: equal steps and a true zero, such as height, income or the number
  of exams taken.

Categories also have to be **exhaustive**, so every unit fits in one, and
**mutually exclusive**, so no unit fits in two. Age groups "18 to 25, 25 to 35"
fail the second test: a 25-year-old fits both.

R stores the level in the column's type. Numbers are `numeric` or `integer`,
categories are `character` or `factor`. R does not know whether your numbers
are ordinal or ratio, so the decision what to compute stays yours.

<CodeBlock id="c-classes" code={`class(starwars$height)
class(starwars$eye_color)
table(starwars$sex)`} />

A count per category, as `table()` gives, works at every level. A mean only
makes sense from interval level upwards.

## From concept to number

A research question uses concepts, such as "stress" or "social support". To
measure one, you first make it precise (**conceptualisation**: what exactly do
we mean, and which dimensions does it have?) and then decide how to observe it
(**operationalisation**: which questions, scale or observation, with which
answer categories?). The result of measuring is a score per unit, which goes in
the data matrix.

The usual **data collection methods** are questionnaires, interviews,
observation, experiments and existing documents or records. Data you collect
yourself for your question is **primary data**. Data someone else collected,
such as a national statistics office or an earlier study, is **secondary
data**: cheaper and often larger, but measured for someone else's purpose.
