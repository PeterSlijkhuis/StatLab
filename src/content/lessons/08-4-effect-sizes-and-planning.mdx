A p-value answers one question: how surprising would data like these be if there were no effect? It does not say how big
the effect is, and when there is a real effect it tends to shrink as the sample grows even though the effect stays
the same. The size of an effect needs its own number, and that number is also
what you need to plan how large a study should be.

<CodeBlock id="c-load" code={`population <- read.csv("data/wellbeing-population.csv", stringsAsFactors = TRUE)
population$sleep_group <- ifelse(population$sleep_hours >= 7, "7 or more", "under 7")

set.seed(8241)
study <- population[sample(nrow(population), 60), ]

a <- study$exam_score[study$sleep_group == "7 or more"]
b <- study$exam_score[study$sleep_group == "under 7"]
mean(a) - mean(b)`} />

This is the study of 60 students from Lessons 8-1 and 8-2. Students who sleep seven hours
or more scored about 7 points higher. Is 7 points a lot? That depends on how
much exam scores vary, and a raw difference does not say.

## Cohen's d

**Cohen's d** divides the difference by the standard deviation within the
groups, so it reads in standard deviations: d = 1 means the groups are one SD
apart. The within-group SD pools the two groups' variances, each weighted by
its degrees of freedom.

<CodeBlock id="c-d" code={`pooled_sd <- sqrt(((length(a) - 1) * var(a) + (length(b) - 1) * var(b)) /
                  (length(a) + length(b) - 2))

d <- (mean(a) - mean(b)) / pooled_sd
d`} />

Cohen's rough benchmarks are 0.2 small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. He offered
them as a last resort, for when a field has nothing better. A d of 0.2 on
mortality is enormous; a d of 0.8 on a lab task nobody cares about is not.

## The effect in the population

This dataset is the whole population, so here, unusually, you can compute the
true effect.

<CodeBlock id="c-true" code={`pa <- population$exam_score[population$sleep_group == "7 or more"]
pb <- population$exam_score[population$sleep_group == "under 7"]
pooled <- sqrt(((length(pa) - 1) * var(pa) + (length(pb) - 1) * var(pb)) /
               (length(pa) + length(pb) - 2))

(mean(pa) - mean(pb)) / pooled`} />

The true d is 0.50. The study found 0.64, and it was significant.

## Planning: how many people?

Lesson 8-3 showed that power depends on the effect size, the sample size and α.
Fix any three of effect, n, α and power, and the fourth follows. `power.t.test()`
solves for whichever one you leave out. With `sd = 1`, `delta` is Cohen's d.

<CodeBlock id="c-power-n" code={`power.t.test(delta = 0.5, sd = 1, sig.level = 0.05, power = 0.8)`} />

About 63.8, so 64 students per group, 128 in all, for 80% power. Always round
up: 63 per group falls just short of the power you asked for.

Leave out `power` instead, and it tells you what a planned design can do.

<CodeBlock id="c-power-of" code={`power.t.test(n = 20, delta = 0.5, sd = 1)`} />

Twenty per group gives about 34% power: two studies in three would miss a
medium effect that is really there.

<Exercise id="m8-4-b" />

Two warnings about planning. First, base the effect size on what matters or on a
body of studies, not on one small significant study, which is likely to
overstate it. Second, `power.t.test()` covers the t-test only; for other designs,
simulate as Lesson 8-3 did, or use a dedicated tool such as G*Power or the
`pwr` package.
