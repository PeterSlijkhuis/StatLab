Lesson 3-4 asked whether a set of items measures **one** thing. Often a
questionnaire is meant to measure several things at once, and the question is
which items belong together. **Exploratory factor analysis** finds the few
underlying factors that explain how a set of items correlate.

As in lesson 3-4, the course data has scale scores rather than items, so this
lesson builds six items of its own, answered from 1 to 7 by 300 people.

<CodeBlock id="c-items" code={`set.seed(172)
n <- 300
pressure <- rnorm(n)
support <- 0.3 * pressure + rnorm(n, 0, 0.95)
item <- function(f, load) round(pmin(7, pmax(1, 4 + 1.3 * (load * f + rnorm(n, 0, sqrt(1 - load^2))))))
items <- data.frame(
  deadlines = item(pressure, 0.80), overtime = item(pressure, 0.75), too_much = item(pressure, 0.70),
  help = item(support, 0.80), listened = item(support, 0.75), feedback = item(support, 0.65)
)

round(cor(items), 2)`} />

The first three items ask about deadlines, overtime and having too much to do.
The last three ask whether a manager helps, listens and gives feedback. In the
correlation table they form two blocks: strong correlations within each set of
three, weak ones between them.

## How many factors?

The **eigenvalues** of the correlation matrix say how much of the items'
variance each successive dimension captures. A **scree plot** draws them, and
the usual reading is to keep the factors before the line flattens.

<CodeBlock id="c-scree" code={`eigenvalues <- eigen(cor(items))$values
round(eigenvalues, 2)

plot(eigenvalues, type = "b", xlab = "Factor", ylab = "Eigenvalue")
abline(h = 1, lty = 2)`} />

Two eigenvalues stand out, 2.34 and 1.65, and after that the line is flat.
The older rule of keeping every eigenvalue above 1 agrees here, though it often
keeps too many. In RStudio, `psych::fa.parallel(items)` runs parallel analysis,
which compares the eigenvalues with those of random data and is the better
guide.

## Fitting the model

`factanal()` is part of base R. Give it the items and the number of factors.

<CodeBlock id="c-efa" code={`efa <- factanal(items, factors = 2, rotation = "promax")

print(efa$loadings, cutoff = 0.3)
efa$PVAL`} />

- Each **loading** is how strongly an item reflects a factor. `cutoff = 0.3`
  hides the small ones, and what is left is the structure: the pressure items on
  one factor, the support items on the other.
- The factors come unnamed. Naming them is your job, from the items that load on
  each.
- The chi-square test asks whether two factors reproduce the correlations. A
  large p-value, here .70 (χ²(4) = 2.22), means the test found no evidence that
  two factors fall short, so two factors are an adequate description.

## Factors or components?

Principal component analysis, `prcomp()`, looks similar and is often confused
with factor analysis.

<CodeBlock id="c-pca" code={`pca <- prcomp(items, scale. = TRUE)
round(summary(pca)$importance["Proportion of Variance", ], 2)`} />

The difference is what they model. Components summarise **all** the variance of
the items, measurement error included, into a few scores. Factors model only the
variance items **share**, as the effect of traits you cannot observe directly.
To reduce many variables to a few scores, use components. To find out which
traits a questionnaire measures, use factors.

## From exploring to confirming

An exploratory analysis finds a structure in one dataset. To test that
structure, specify it in advance and fit a **confirmatory factor analysis** to
new data. In RStudio, with the `lavaan` package:

```r
library(lavaan)
model <- "
  pressure =~ deadlines + overtime + too_much
  support  =~ help + listened + feedback
"
fit <- cfa(model, data = items)
summary(fit, fit.measures = TRUE, standardized = TRUE)
```

Common guides for good fit are a CFI of .95 or more, an RMSEA of .06 or less and
an SRMR of .08 or less.
